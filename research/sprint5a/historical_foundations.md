## 1. Phenotype definition and above-level testing (SMPY)

**How SMPY selected its participants** (Lubinski & Benbow 2006, p. 317):

- Four of the five cohorts were selected at about age 12–13.
- The first screen was the top 3% on a grade-level achievement test.
- The SAT, a test built for students 4–5 years older, was then given as **above-level testing**.
- For these students the SAT functions as analytical reasoning rather than a test of taught content, because they had no formal algebra.

**Grade-level tests have ceilings that are too low** (p. 318):

- The scores of able students "pile up at the top", which masks their profile of strengths and weaknesses.
- **Restriction of range from ceiling effects is therefore a measurement problem before it is a genetic one.**

**The cohort definitions** are in Table 1 (p. 319). The two cohorts below matter most here.

| Cohort | What it is |
|---|---|
| 3 | SAT-M ≥ 700 or SAT-V ≥ 630 before age 13: roughly the **top 1 in 10,000**, national in scope |
| 5 | 1st- and 2nd-year graduate students in top-ranked US engineering, maths and science departments in 1992: selected on accomplishment, not on a test |
| 1 | selection cut-offs ≈ top 1% |
| 4 | top 3%, with a subset meeting top-0.5% SAT criteria |

**Individual differences within the top 1% are large and predictive** (p. 318, pp. 320–322):

- **Learning rate.** Students scoring ≥ 500 before age 13 (top 1 in 200) can assimilate a high-school course in about 3 weeks. Those scoring ≥ 700 (top 1 in 10,000) assimilate at least twice that.
- **Doctorates.** About 30% versus 50% earned doctorates, against a 1% base rate.
- **Quartiles within the top 1%.** Comparing the top and bottom quartiles still separates later doctorates, patents, income and tenure (Wai et al. 2005; Fig. 2, p. 321).
- **Cohort 3 against Cohort 5.** Cohort 3 participants, identified by one test at 13, matched or exceeded Cohort 5 graduate students on tenure, full professorship and income by their mid-30s (Fig. 3, p. 322).

**Specific abilities** (pp. 316, 323–325, 329–331):

- The **level and pattern** of mathematical, verbal and **spatial** ability predict favourite courses, college major and occupation.
- Math and spatial ability tilt people towards STEM; verbal ability towards the humanities and social sciences.
- Talent searches miss about **50% of the top 1% in 3-D spatial visualisation** (p. 323).
- Spatial ability added about 2.4% incremental validity after the SAT plus interests and values (p. 331).

**The authors name the gaps themselves:**

- Spatial ability was not measured at selection.
- The measurement must reach beyond a single scalar g.

## 2. Restriction of range and "giftedness = g?" (Detterman 1993, pp. 22–43)

**Restriction of range** (pp. 24–25, Figs 1–2):

- Selecting an extreme group shrinks the standard deviation and attenuates correlations.
- A sample correlation of 0.1 among the highly selected can correspond to a much larger population correlation.
- **Caution is therefore required with extreme-group studies.**

**Intelligence is probably an important cause of exceptional ability** (pp. 25–26):

- Within selected groups, personality and motivation explain differences among people *matched* on ability.
- They do not show that intelligence is unimportant. Detterman's illustration is admitting graduate students from the full IQ range.

**Heritability across the ability range** (p. 29):

- Claims of different heritability at different IQ levels have produced "every possible outcome".
- Settling the question needs large samples.

**Discussion (pp. 35–37).** Garrett's differentiation idea was raised in the discussion: g matters more at lower ability and younger ages, and specialised abilities matter more among the more able. Detterman's reply was that, without an unselected sample, you cannot conclude that selection on intelligence is unimportant.

## 3. Mathematically talented profiles (Benbow & Lubinski 1993, pp. 44–66)

**"Gifted" should be continuous, not a category** (p. 45):

- The top 1% of the IQ distribution spans roughly 137 to over 200.
- That span is about as wide as the range from the bottom 2% to the top 2%.

**Individual differences within the top 1% are seldom observed** (p. 47), for three reasons:

1. Out-of-level testing is needed.
2. Top-1% samples are small.
3. The outcome criteria lack ceilings.

**Table 1 (p. 46).** Within 2,000 top-1% children, the top quartile beats the bottom quartile on achievement criteria.

**Sex differences** (pp. 44, 55–57):

- Differences in SAT-M go with differences in spatial-mechanical reasoning and in preferences.
- Possible biological linkages were offered **speculatively** (p. 56 onwards). These are hypotheses and have no molecular support.

## 4. Genetics and high cognitive ability (Plomin & Thompson 1993, pp. 67–84)

**Heritability in the normal range does not imply heritability of the high extreme** (pp. 67, 70–71).

- **The DeFries–Fulker extreme-group analysis** (early twin data) suggested that high IQ is about as heritable as normal-range differences.
- Hence the hypothesis on p. 72: high ability "may be merely the extreme of the normal continuum of g". This is the **continuity hypothesis**.

**The molecular strategy** (pp. 73–77):

- Many genes of small effect are expected, i.e. quantitative trait loci (QTL) (p. 74).
- Allelic association in unrelated individuals is preferred to linkage for small effects (pp. 75–76).
- Their design compared extreme-high and average IQ groups. The replication sample included 30 children with mean IQ 142 (p. 77).
- They screened more than 20 candidate DNA markers: DRD1, DRD3, MAOB, neurofilament and fragile-X repeats. They agreed not to publish until replicated (p. 77).
- They were specifically interested in finding **"increasing" alleles** (p. 77).

**What they expected and what they left unresolved** (p. 83):

- Plomin predicted "a DNA marker or two that account for a tiny amount of IQ variance".
- Selecting people 3 SD above the mean could yield large allele-frequency differences that still explain little population variance.
- Unresolved in 1993:
  - whether the extreme is aetiologically continuous with the normal range;
  - the genetics of **specific** abilities at the high end ("little is known", p. 67);
  - which genes are involved.
